Funeral Order of Service: A Simple Order to Follow

A typical funeral runs 30 to 60 minutes in this order: prelude music, welcome, opening prayer or words, a reading, a song, the eulogy, remarks from family or friends, closing prayer or words, and the recessional.

A common order

  1. Prelude (5–10 min): soft music while guests sit down
  2. Processional: family and casket come in, if your service has one
  3. Welcome (2–3 min): the officiant greets everyone and says why you are gathered
  4. Opening prayer or words (1–2 min)
  5. Reading (2–3 min): scripture, a poem or a short passage
  6. Song or hymn (3–5 min): sung together, by a soloist, or played
  7. Eulogy (5–10 min): one person tells the life story
  8. Remarks (2–3 min each): two or three family members or friends
  9. Slideshow or moment of silence (optional)
  10. Closing prayer or words (1–2 min)
  11. Recessional: music as the family and casket leave first

How to write it in the program

Put each part on its own line with the name of the person leading it to the right, like “Eulogy ........ Michael Bennett, son.” Use song titles, not full lyrics, unless you want guests to sing along. Leave out the minutes; they are for planning, not for guests.

Who decides the order

The officiant or funeral director usually has a standard order. Start with theirs, then add or swap the parts that matter to your family. A Catholic funeral Mass has a fixed order of its own, so check with the parish before you print.

For a non-religious service

Swap the prayers for opening and closing words, and the scripture for a poem or a favorite passage. The rest of the order stays the same.

Quick answers

What is the order of service at a funeral?

Usually prelude music, a welcome, an opening prayer or words, a reading, a song, the eulogy, remarks from family or friends, closing words, and the recessional.

How long is a funeral service?

Most run 30 to 60 minutes. A full Catholic funeral Mass often runs about an hour.

Do you put the eulogy speaker's name in the program?

Yes. Most programs list the name of each person who reads, sings or speaks, often with how they knew the person who died.
